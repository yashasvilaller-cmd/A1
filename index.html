01

Computer characteristics & generations

Remember · List and identify

Four primary characteristics of a modern digital computer

  • Speed: performs millions or billions of operations quickly.
  • Accuracy: gives correct results when data and instructions are correct.
  • Storage: saves large amounts of data and retrieves it when needed.
  • Automation: follows stored instructions with little ongoing human intervention.

Five generations of computers

GenerationMain hardware technologyKey change
1stVacuum tubesVery large, hot, high power use
2ndTransistorsSmaller, faster, more reliable
3rdIntegrated circuits (ICs)More components on compact chips
4thMicroprocessors (VLSI)Personal computers and compact systems
5thULSI, parallel processing and AI-oriented systemsGreater intelligent and high-performance computing

Functional block diagram of a computer

Understand · Explain the flow

A computer accepts input, processes it under the control of instructions, stores data, and presents results.

Input unit
→
CPU
→
Output unit
Memory / Storage
↔
CPU

Control signals coordinate all units; data and instructions move along system buses.

What each unit does

  • Input unit: enters data and instructions (keyboard, scanner, microphone).
  • CPU: the Central Processing Unit. The ALU performs arithmetic/logic; the Control Unit directs operations.
  • Memory: RAM temporarily holds active data and instructions; storage keeps files long-term.
  • Output unit: communicates results through a monitor, printer, speaker or other device.
Flow: Input → memory/CPU processing → results stored if needed → output. Control signals coordinate timing and actions.

Scanning devices & electronic card readers

Apply · Real-world implementation

Campus example: use scanning and card-reading hardware to record attendance and manage library checkout.

Student ID / Book
→
Scanner or reader
→
Campus system
→
Record updated

Optical scanning devices

  • Barcode scanner: reads printed bars/spaces on student IDs or books.
  • OMR scanner: detects filled bubbles on forms, useful for surveys or tests.

Electronic card readers

  • RFID: reads a compatible radio-frequency tag without direct contact, within range.
  • Smart-card reader: reads data from a chip card, often through contact or supported contactless technology.

Attendance: student presents an ID → reader captures ID → system checks enrolment and timestamp → attendance record is saved.

Library checkout: scan student card and book ID → system verifies item → loan record and due date are saved.

Use unique IDs, access permissions, backup records and a manual fallback for damaged cards or scanner failure.

Languages differ in how close they are to machine hardware and how much translation is needed.

05

Point-and-draw devices vs digitizers

Analyze · Compare precision and operation
DeviceHow it tracks inputTypical use
MouseRelative movement across a surface moves the pointerGeneral selection, drawing and editing
TrackballUser rotates a ball; sensors measure its movementPrecise pointer control in limited desk space
Graphics tablet / digitizerPen or puck position is mapped to tablet coordinates; pressure/tilt may be supportedCAD drafting, illustration and digital art

Why the mechanisms differ

  • Mouse: reports relative motion, so the cursor can be repositioned repeatedly.
  • Trackball: ball rotation controls pointer movement without moving the whole device.
  • Digitizer: can capture more direct, fine-grained coordinates; a pressure-sensitive stylus can vary line width or brush effects.
CAD: a digitizer may suit precise tracing and coordinate entry. Digital art: pen pressure and tilt can make strokes feel more natural. Performance also depends on resolution, calibration, software and user skill.
06

Plotter vs large-format printer

Evaluate · Assess technical trade-offs

Scenario: producing civil-engineering blueprints and large technical drawings.

FactorDrum/flatbed plotterHigh-resolution large-format printer
Line precisionTraditionally excellent vector line controlModern models can produce very fine lines and detailed output
OutputOften optimized for CAD/vector drawingsCan handle line drawings, filled areas, images and colour graphics
ScalabilityDepends on model, media and workflowWide range of roll and sheet widths
Long-term costConsider maintenance, parts, media and specialist supportConsider ink/toner, printheads, media, maintenance and volume

Recommendation

For most current engineering offices, a modern large-format printer with CAD/vector support is a practical choice because it can combine accurate line work with versatile output. A specialist plotter can be preferable where a particular workflow requires it.

Choose by verified line accuracy, resolution, paper width, driver/CAD compatibility, throughput, service availability and total cost of ownership—not by the device name alone.
07

Facial recognition vs voice recognition

Evaluate · Reliability and security critique

Both are biometric technologies, and both can be affected by their environment. Neither should be treated as perfectly reliable on its own.

IssueFacial recognitionVoice recognition
Environmental effectsPoor lighting, camera angle, masks or image quality may reduce accuracyBackground noise, echo, distance and microphone quality can reduce accuracy
Presentation attacksPhotos, video or masks may challenge weak systems; liveness checks helpRecordings or synthesized/cloned voices may challenge weak systems; anti-spoofing helps
PrivacyCamera capture may feel intrusive; face data is sensitiveVoice recordings can reveal identity and other personal information
Public-space concernCan identify people without active participation if deployed carelesslyCan be overheard or captured in shared/noisy spaces

Safer deployment

  • Use multi-factor authentication for important access.
  • Enable liveness/anti-spoofing and rate limits where appropriate.
  • Encrypt biometric templates, restrict access and set retention limits.
  • Test performance across varied users and real environmental conditions.
  • Provide a non-biometric alternative and a clear appeal/fallback process.
Conclusion: in a noisy public setting, voice accuracy may fall; in poor lighting or with face coverings, facial accuracy may fall. Select after a local risk assessment rather than assuming one is always superior.

Accessible, hands-free workstation

Create · Design an assistive system

Goal: enable a visually impaired user to operate a computer with minimal reliance on sight, combining non-visual input with speech and tactile output.

Voice commands
+ tactile keyboard
→
Computer
+ accessibility software
→
Screen reader
+ speech synthesizer
Headphones / speakers
↔
Braille display (optional)

Input devices

  • Voice recognition: opens applications, dictates text and runs supported commands; use a good microphone and quiet setup.
  • Tactile keyboard: raised key markers or a familiar keyboard layout support touch typing and keyboard shortcuts.
  • Optional switches: alternative access devices can help users who have additional motor needs.

Output devices

  • Screen reader: reads interface text, menus, notifications and document content aloud.
  • Voice-response synthesizer: turns computer text into speech through headphones or speakers.
  • Refreshable Braille display: provides tactile Braille output for users who read Braille.

Setup checklist

  • Enable screen reader at sign-in and set preferred speech rate/voice.
  • Configure keyboard navigation and clear shortcut guides.
  • Use accessible apps, labelled controls and high-contrast/zoom options when useful.
  • Provide headphones for privacy, a backup input method and regular training.
  • Protect voice data and obtain consent before recording or cloud processing.
Design principle: offer more than one input and output method, because users' needs and the accessibility of individual applications differ.

Computers process data according to instructions. Different input devices capture different forms of information; output devices present results in suitable forms. The best technology is the one that meets the task's needs for accuracy, speed, cost, security and accessibility.
